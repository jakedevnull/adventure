# The Crossroads, Three Ages

max_rooms: 8

## Story

### The author's story, carried over

> The Turning House has been seen; now the ground it stands on. The landlady of 2099 BA
> holds that the House is only half of what the family keeps, and the other half is the
> crossroads it was built to watch. Tonight the door is unbarred for you. Step **OUT**.
>
> This story is about walking as much as striding. Outside the door is a yard at the meeting
> of two high roads, with a milestone at the centre of the crossing and a well against the
> House's east wall. From the yard the player can walk **EAST** along the road to the
> milestone and **IN** to come back to the common room; the years run from the yard as they
> run from the House. Show the same ground in three ages: the High Masonry (2099 BA), when the
> milestone is new-cut and the roads carry everything; the Morning Country (99 AA), when the
> grass has the road and the milestone lies where it fell; and the Lettered Age (2099 AA),
> when the crossing is surveyed, the well is capped, and the milestone has a label.
>
> The through-line is the milestone. Its letters are the same in every age; what changes is
> who can read them and what they think they say. Nobody explains the Gap. The narrator
> reports what is on the stone and what the people beside it make of it, and stops.
>
> Requirements the plan must honour:
>
> * At least two places outside the House, each reached by a **spatial** exit (OUT from the
>   common room, then a compass direction), not by time alone. A player who never types
>   FUTURE should still be able to see the yard and the road in one age.
> * The yard's faces open to PAST and FUTURE so the player can walk out in one age and stride
>   from there. The road may open in fewer ages if the plan wants it.
> * The existing common-room faces gain an OUT exit in the ages the story uses and are
>   otherwise left as they are.
> * Tone: dry on the surface, deep underneath. The cold, the grass, the survey pegs are
>   information, not mood.

### What this story is

The first story showed one room in six ages. This one opens the door and shows the ground.
It is the same move made outward: the Turning House is a place that persists, and the reason
it persists is the crossing it was built to watch. The player walks out of the inn into the
yard, walks east to the milestone, and discovers that the stone is the only witness in the
game who has been present the whole time and never changed its mind.

The player is not solving anything. They are reading a stone in three ages and watching what
the living do with it. That is the whole game of it, and it is enough: the letters hold, the
readers don't.

### What the player is trying to do, and why

Nothing is locked and nothing is won. The landlady opened the door; the pull is curiosity,
and the story rewards it with a stone whose meaning decays while its text does not. The
player is the only person in Everwyn who will ever know what the milestone says, because
they were standing there when it was cut. That knowledge is the payload. It is never stated
by the narrator; it is assembled by the player out of three rooms.

The one thing the player *does* is carry a reading forward. The `read` text on the milestone
at 2099 BA and the `read` text on the milestone at 99 AA are **the same string, word for
word**. In 2099 AA a University placard quotes those same letters and then explains them
wrongly, at length and with confidence. The joke is structural and the player is the only
one in on it.

### The arc

**Open.** Step OUT of the common room in the High Masonry into a cold yard at the meeting of
two high roads. The roads are new and carrying everything. A well stands against the House's
east wall with a rope on it. East, at the centre of the crossing, a man is finishing the
letters on a milestone.

**Turn.** The years run from the yard as they run from the House. The player strides forward
on one patch of ground — the Long Noon, the Hush, over the Gap — and comes down in the
Morning Country, where the grass has the roads and the milestone is lying in it. A girl who
cannot read tells the player what her people say the stone means. She is wrong in an
affectionate way. The letters have not moved.

**Resolve.** Two more strides bring the player to the Lettered Age. The crossing is surveyed
and pegged, the well is capped with an iron plate, and a post beside the yard carries a
printed placard about the milestone. The placard quotes the letters exactly and is wrong in
a scholarly way. The narrator does not correct anybody. The player goes back in for the fire.

### The beats, in order

1. **2099 BA · the High Masonry · the yard.** OUT. Cold that the season has not earned, the
   same cold as inside. Two high roads crossing, new-laid, carrying carts at an hour when
   roads should be empty. The well against the east wall, open, a rope on it, cold water.
   East along the road, someone is working by lamplight.
2. **2099 BA · the High Masonry · the crossing.** EAST. A milestone, new-cut, still pale
   where the chisel went. A road-mason finishing the last letter. He reads it out because
   reading it out is nothing to him; it is a distance and a town. He is more interested in
   the fit of the road than in the stone.
3. **1099 BA · the Long Noon · the yard.** FUTURE. The same ground, fat with traffic. Wine
   going one way, wool the other, and a carter waiting on a gate with a map that is wrong
   about the river. The well has a windlass on it now. Nobody walks east; there is no reason
   to look at a milestone you have passed all your life.
4. **99 BA · the Hush · the yard.** FUTURE. The roads are empty in both directions, which
   has never happened. No lights in any window, the House included. The bucket is in the
   well and the rope coiled. The door behind you is shut. Menace delivered as weather.
5. **99 AA · the Morning Country · the yard.** FUTURE — the stride over the Gap, one step,
   no explanation offered and none available. Grass has the roads. The drystone wellhead
   stands exactly as it was laid; the mortared work along the road is a heap of stone and
   lime. There are houses here because there is water here.
6. **99 AA · the Morning Country · the crossing.** EAST, through grass, along a road you can
   feel underfoot and not see. The milestone lies where it fell. A girl is sitting on it.
   She cannot read, nobody she knows can read, and she tells the player what the stone is
   for. It is a good answer and it is not the answer.
7. **1099 AA · the Rekindling · the yard.** FUTURE. The canals took the traffic and the yard
   is a yard again — a place to turn a cart, not a place to pass through. A pump-handle has
   been set over the well. The roads are still under the grass and still the fastest way
   anywhere, and nobody is in a hurry.
8. **2099 AA · the Lettered Age · the yard.** FUTURE. Survey pegs in a line across the
   crossing, string between them. The well capped with a bolted iron plate, its depth cut
   into the cap. A post by the wall with a printed placard on it. The milestone stands
   upright again at the centre of the crossing, cleaned, with its letters legible and its
   meaning gone. A student is copying the placard into a notebook rather than walking over
   and looking at the stone. Then IN, to the fire and the lamps and the one empty hook.

### Places and eras

Two new places, both outside the House, both reached by spatial exits.

**`crossroads-yard`** — the House's dooryard at the corner of the crossing. This is the
place that persists: it has a face at **every one of the six landings**, so the years run
from it exactly as they run from the House. That is not decoration, it is the requirement:
strides move between faces of the same place at **adjacent** landings, so a yard that opens
PAST and FUTURE from the High Masonry to the Lettered Age must exist at 1099 BA, 99 BA and
1099 AA too. Those three faces are the ground the stride crosses. They are not filler — the
Long Noon's traffic, the Hush's empty roads and the Rekindling's canals are canon, and each
one shows the roads doing something different — but they are shorter, and they carry no
milestone.

**`high-crossing`** — the centre of the crossing, one step EAST of the yard, where the
milestone stands. This is the road, and the author's permission to open it "in fewer ages"
is taken: it has **two** faces, 2099 BA and 99 AA. Its time exits are **closed in both**.
The loose ground is the House and its dooryard; the middle of the crossing is not loose.
Nobody remarks on this and the narrator never explains it. Mechanically it means the player
walks east, looks, and walks back west — walking, not striding, which is what the story is
about.

The Lettered Age's reading of the stone happens from the yard, on the placard post, with the
milestone visible at the centre of the crossing. The author's three 2099 AA details — the
surveyed crossing, the capped well, the labelled milestone — are all visible from the yard,
and the placard is the thing with words on it.

The player never has to type FUTURE to see both places: in 2099 BA, OUT then EAST does it.

### The milestone, exactly

The stone carries two lines of letters and nothing else:

```
ELLERMARK
NINE
```

Ellermark is a town nine miles east in 2099 BA. Both facts are ordinary in 2099 BA and both
are gone by 99 AA — the town, the road-measure, and the habit of writing distances on stone.

The letters are the through-line. Write them **once** and reuse the identical string:

- **2099 BA, `high-crossing`.** The milestone is an `Item` (`takeable: false`) with `read`
  giving the two lines. The road-mason's `talk` says what it means, flatly, the way you would
  say the time: Ellermark, nine, and a remark about the road rather than the stone.
- **99 AA, `high-crossing`.** The milestone is an `Item` (`takeable: false`) with `read`
  giving **the same two lines, byte for byte**, weathered but whole. The girl's `talk` gives
  her people's reading: the stone counts something — the dead, the years, the houses that
  were here — and *nine* is the count. She is certain and she is kind about it.
- **2099 AA, `crossroads-yard`.** The milestone is `Scenery` (one sentence: upright, cleaned,
  letters unchanged). The **placard** is the `Item` with `read`: it quotes `ELLERMARK / NINE`
  exactly and then glosses it — Ellermark as an administrative district otherwise unattested,
  *nine* as an ordinal in a numbered series of boundary markers, with a note that the
  sequence's other eight stones have not been found. Museum-placard prose in small capitals,
  like the labelled case already in `turning-house:2099-aa`. Confident, careful, wrong.

Nobody in any age says the word "Gap" and nobody explains why a town stopped existing.

### The well

The second through-line, and the universe's own line made literal: *not the cathedral, but
the well beside it.* It stands against the House's east wall in every yard face, and it is
the only reason there is a village in the Morning Country. It never becomes a puzzle. It is
scenery in all six faces, one sentence each, and what changes is the gear bolted to it:

| Landing | The well |
| --- | --- |
| 2099 BA | Open, a rope, cold water. |
| 1099 BA | A windlass on it, brass, better than it needs to be. |
| 99 BA | Bucket in, rope coiled. Nobody is drawing tonight. |
| 99 AA | The drystone head stands as laid; the mortared work along the road is a heap. |
| 1099 AA | A pump-handle set over it, and the rope kept anyway. |
| 2099 AA | Capped with a bolted iron plate, the depth cut into the cap. |

### People

Four, across eight rooms, and never two in a room. Everwyn must not feel crowded. All are
`Scenery` with a `talk` line (the engine has no NPC type; scenery carrying `talk` is how it
is done).

- **The road-mason**, `high-crossing:2099-ba`. Finishing the last letter. Cares about the
  road's fit, not the stone's meaning. Reads the letters out as a courtesy, the way you would
  give directions.
- **The carter**, `crossroads-yard:1099-ba`. Waiting on a gate with a map that is wrong about
  the river — the Long Noon in one object. Has passed the milestone a thousand times and
  could not tell you what it says.
- **The girl**, `high-crossing:99-aa`. Sitting on the fallen stone. Cannot read; neither can
  anyone she knows; does not experience this as a lack. Her reading of the stone is her
  village's reading and she offers it as common knowledge.
- **The student**, `crossroads-yard:2099-aa`. Copying the placard into a notebook with their
  back to the milestone. One dry line. Not a satire of scholarship — just someone reading
  the label because the label is what is meant to be read.

The landlady stays inside and unedited. The story's framing — that the House is half of what
the family keeps and the crossing is the other half — is carried by the door standing
unbarred and by the yard itself, not by new dialogue in an existing room.

### Tone notes

Dry on the surface, deep underneath. The cold, the grass, the survey pegs are **information,
not mood** — a survey peg is a survey peg, not a symbol of anything, and the narrator must
not know it is a symbol of anything. No line concludes on the player's behalf. Nobody
explains the Gap, the Lapse, or why a town is gone; the narrator reports what is on the
stone and what the people beside it make of it, and stops.

Era inflection is in the **nouns** only: windlass, pump-handle, iron cap, survey peg, string
line. One narrator, one voice, first landing to last. No pastiche grammar anywhere, including
in `talk` lines — the girl and the mason speak plainly; era colour is in what they know, not
how they conjugate.

History in one clause, never a paragraph. The Morning Country face does not narrate a
collapse; it says the grass has the roads and moves on.

### What the engine can and cannot do here

Binding constraints, confirmed against `src/types.ts`, `src/parser.ts`, `src/world.ts` and
`src/voice.ts`. Build within them rather than rediscovering them.

- **A stride needs a face at the adjacent landing.** `PAST`/`FUTURE` move to the same `place`
  at the neighbouring entry in `world.landings`. If `time.future` is `true` and no face of
  that place exists at the next landing, the player gets *"You reach on. The years give — but
  nothing of this place stands in that age, and they set you down where you were."* and does
  not move. **This plan has no such case**; keep it that way.
- **Spatial exits are never auto-listed.** The engine prints only its own time line. If the
  prose does not mention a door or a road, the player will never find the exit. Weave exits
  into the `look`, per the writing guide's room template — never a compass line.
- **Exits are not reciprocal automatically.** Every `east` needs its matching `west`, every
  `out` its matching `in`.
- **No world state, no flags, no conditional text.** An item's `description`/`read` is one
  fixed string for the whole game. Do not build anything that depends on what the player
  carries. In particular: the brass lamp travels, but there is no `LIGHT` verb and nothing
  can react to it.
- **Verbs that exist:** look, examine, take, drop, inventory, go, past, future, when, wait,
  again, say, talk, mark, read, eat, help, quit. Directions include `in`/`out`. There is no
  `OPEN`, `PUT`, `GIVE`, `UNLOCK`, `CLIMB` or `DRAW`. Do not write a well that wants drawing.
- **`read` is an `Item` field only.** `Scenery` has `description` and `talk`. Anything the
  player must read has to be an `Item` (use `takeable: false` with a dry `takeRefusal`).
- **Item ids are globally unique.** Taken already: `lamp`, `bread`, `coin`, `long-noon-map`,
  `lintel`, `card`, `case`. Suffix new ones by landing.
- **The room heading prints `title · landing` itself**, and the time line is appended
  automatically. Never announce the year in `look`.
- **Voice lint (`npm run eval:voice`) hard-fails on:** `look`/`lookAgain` over 4 sentences
  (the object-listing line counts, and a `\n` does not reset the count); any `description`
  over 1 sentence; more than one em-dash per passage; **any** exclamation mark; the
  `is not just/merely/only/simply` reversal; and the banned words *mysterious, eerie,
  ominous, strange, load-bearing, leverage, robust, delve, tapestry, landscape, elevate,
  resonate, testament to, at its core*.
- **`world.landings` needs no change.** All six landings already exist.
- **`scripts/play.ts` drops the first command when `--expect` is absent.** Always publish
  routes as `node scripts/play.ts --expect <room id> "CMD" "CMD" …`.

## Through-lines

- **The milestone's letters** (`high-crossing:2099-ba` → `high-crossing:99-aa` →
  `crossroads-yard:2099-aa`) — the `read` string `ELLERMARK / NINE` is **identical** in the
  first two rooms and quoted verbatim inside the placard's `read` in the third. Write it once
  and copy it. Three readers: the mason who knows, the girl who cannot read, the University
  that can read and is wrong. The narrator never adjudicates.
- **The well** (all six `crossroads-yard` faces) — scenery, one sentence each, against the
  House's east wall in every age. What changes is the gear on it: rope, windlass, coiled rope,
  bare drystone head, pump-handle, bolted iron cap. It is the reason there is a village in
  99 AA and it never becomes a puzzle.
- **The roads** (all eight rooms) — the High Roads are a fixed point laid in 2099 BA. Each
  face shows them doing something, and no face repeats the road line the matching
