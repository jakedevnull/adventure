# The Eighth Stone

max_rooms: 8

## Story

### The author's story, carried over

> The milestone at the crossing says ELLERMARK / NINE, and the University of the Lettered Age
> holds that its other eight stones have never been found. This story walks the player one
> mile further up the eastern road, to the next place the road has to deal with: the river.
>
> From the centre of the crossing, **EAST** along the road brings the player to the ford,
> where the eastern high road meets the river that the Long Noon's map could not place. The
> ford is a place, and it persists: stepping stones and a ferry-post in the High Masonry
> (2099 BA), when the road is new and someone collects a toll; silt and a plank in the
> Morning Country (99 AA), when nobody remembers what the stones were for; a lock, a
> lock-keeper's cottage and the canal tolls in the Rekindling (1099 AA); and in the Lettered
> Age (2099 AA), whatever the plan decides a surveyed river looks like. The years run from
> the ford as they run from the yard. The road between the crossing and the ford may be a
> place of its own in an age or two, if the budget allows and the walk wants a middle.
>
> The through-line is the eighth stone. Somewhere at the ford, in every age, there is a
> milestone that reads ELLERMARK / EIGHT, in the same hand as the one at the crossing. It is
> in the water, or under the plank, or built into the lock wall, or on a plinth with a label
> that says something else. Nobody in any age says the word "eight" out loud, and the
> narrator does not connect it to anything. The player carries the connection.
>
> Requirements the plan must honour:
>
> * The ford is reached by walking: **EAST** from `high-crossing` in the ages where the
>   crossing exists (2099 BA and 99 AA), with a matching **WEST** back. A player who never
>   types FUTURE can walk from the common room to the river in 2099 BA.
> * The ford's faces open to PAST and FUTURE, so the ages the crossing does not exist in are
>   reached by striding at the ford. Every face must still be reachable; check the chain.
> * The river is the edge of the map. There is no crossing it, no SWIM, no CROSS, no boat
>   that goes anywhere: an attempt to go further east gets a dry refusal in voice, not a
>   new room.
> * Existing rooms: the `high-crossing` faces gain an `east` exit and nothing else changes in
>   them. The yard and the House are not touched.
> * Tone as before: dry on the surface, deep underneath. Water level, toll prices and what
>   the lock-keeper charges are information, not mood.

### What this story is

The first story showed one room in six ages. The second opened the door and walked east to a
milestone. This one keeps walking. A mile past the crossing the road runs out of dry ground,
and the story is about what six ages do with a river and what none of them do with a stone.

The previous story ended on a University placard that quotes ELLERMARK / NINE, glosses it
wrongly, and adds that the sequence's other eight stones have not been found. That placard is
already in the game, at `crossroads-yard:2099-aa`, and it is the trap this story springs. One
mile east, in every age including the Lettered one, the eighth stone is standing in plain
sight with its letters legible. The Lettered Age has it, has cleaned it, has put it on a
plinth, and has labelled it as something else entirely.

The player is not solving anything. There is no puzzle, no item to carry, no lock to open.
The player reads two stones a mile apart, hears six people talk about water and money, and
is the only person in Everwyn who ever puts the two stones in the same sentence. The
narrator never puts them in the same sentence. That restraint is the story.

### What the player is trying to do, and why

Curiosity, again, and the story pays it in the same coin: a fact the whole world has and
nobody holds. The pull is the road. The mason at the crossing in 2099 BA already says, in
text that is written and shipped, *"Nine miles, and the last two of them are mine."* Two
miles, two stones. The ninth is under his hands. The eighth is one mile east, and he cut it
last week. Nothing in the game says so. The player works it out by walking there.

The one thing the player *does* is carry a reading forward, exactly as in the previous
story. The eighth stone's `read` text is **the same string in all six ford faces** —
`ELLERMARK` and `EIGHT`, on two lines, in the same hand as the ninth. Six ages find six uses
for that stone: a bollard, a gauge, a plank-rest, a course in a lock wall, an exhibit. None
of them is reading.

### The arc

**Open.** From the centre of the crossing in the High Masonry, walk EAST. The road is a week
old and the land begins to fall. A mile of it, and then water: stepping stones, a ferry-post,
a man with a box who wants a coin for the crossing. A new stone stands at the head of the
stepping stones with its foot in the river, and the letters are the letters.

**Turn.** The years run from the ford. The player strides forward on one patch of bank — the
Long Noon, where the ferry is a lease and the river is on two maps in two places; the Hush,
where the water is the lowest anyone has seen it and there is nobody at the ford to say so;
over the Gap into the Morning Country, where a plank rests one end on the stone and a woman
setting an eel-trap calls it the plank stone. Nobody has said eight. Nobody will.

**Resolve.** Two more strides. In the Rekindling the river has a lock, a cottage and a book
of tolls, and the stone is a course in the lock's near wall with the keeper reading the water
off its top line. In the Lettered Age the stone has been dug out of that wall, scrubbed, set
on a plinth by the towpath, and given a label that dates it by the place it was found in and
calls its second line a mason's tally. The label is careful, sourced and wrong, and it stands
one mile east of a placard that says the other eight stones have never been found. The player
walks back west, or strides, or stands there.

### The beats, in order

1. **2099 BA · the High Masonry · the east road.** EAST from the crossing. A mile of new
   road, laid this season, with the spoil still heaped along the verge and the land beginning
   to tilt. Nobody on it. You hear the water before the road shows it to you.
2. **2099 BA · the High Masonry · the ford.** EAST. The road walks into the river and comes
   out the other side. Stepping stones, a ferry-post with a toll-board nailed to it, and a
   man with a box. A milestone stands new-cut at the head of the stones, its foot under
   water at this level. He takes the coin, tells you the price and the depth, and has no
   opinion at all about the stone.
3. **1099 BA · the Long Noon · the ford.** FUTURE. The river has moved and the stone is four
   feet out in the shallows, green to the waterline. The ferry is a lease now, held by a man
   who is prosperous on it and who runs the boat only at high water, which this is not. He
   reads the water off the stone twice a day and has never once read the stone.
4. **99 BA · the Hush · the ford.** FUTURE. The water is the lowest it has been in living
   memory and the whole stone is out of it, foot and all, for the first time in an age.
   There is nobody at the ford. The box is on the post and it is empty, and the boat is
   pulled up and dry. A bell goes somewhere west and nothing answers it.
5. **99 AA · the Morning Country · the ford.** FUTURE — the stride over the Gap, one step,
   nothing offered by way of explanation. Silt has taken the stepping stones and a plank
   has been laid across the narrows, with its near end resting on the fallen milestone. A
   woman is setting an eel-trap. She tells you what the plank stone is for. It is a good
   answer and it is not the answer.
6. **99 AA · the Morning Country · the east road.** WEST. The road back to the crossing is a
   drovers' path in the grass that follows the buried paving exactly and does not know it,
   with a hedge grown along one side of it. This is where the walk is a walk.
7. **1099 AA · the Rekindling · the ford.** Back EAST and FUTURE (or straight FUTURE from the
   ford, which is the same step). The river has a lock in it, a cottage beside it and a book
   of tolls in the cottage. The stone is a course in the near wall of the lock chamber, face
   out, at the waterline, and the keeper charges by what the water is doing against it. He
   quotes the rate. He does not quote the stone.
8. **2099 AA · the Lettered Age · the ford.** FUTURE. The lock is kept in order for one boat
   a week. A gauge board, a benchmark cut into the coping, level pegs along the bank. The
   stone is out of the wall and up on a plinth beside the towpath, scrubbed, with a printed
   label on it that has a date, a provenance and a reading, and gets the reading wrong. There
   is nobody here. The letters are legible from where you are standing.

### Places and eras

Two new places. Eight new rooms, which is the budget exactly.

**`ford`** — where the eastern high road meets the river, one mile east of the crossing. This
is the place that persists, and it takes the author's line "the years run from the ford as
they run from the yard" literally: it has a face at **every one of the six landings**, with
PAST and FUTURE open everywhere except the two ends of the world. That is not generosity, it
is arithmetic. A stride moves to the same place at the **adjacent** landing only. The ford
can only be walked to in 2099 BA and 99 AA, so 1099 BA, 99 BA, 1099 AA and 2099 AA are
reached by striding *at the ford* — and the stride from 2099 BA to 1099 AA passes through
1099 BA, 99 BA and 99 AA whether the story wants those ages or not. The chain is the
requirement; the faces are the chain.

They are not filler. The Long Noon's ferry lease is the age that has the best wine and the
worst maps making money off a river it cannot draw. The Hush's low water is the age's dread
delivered as a measurement. Each of those two faces is shorter than the four the author
named, and each shows the river doing something different.

**`east-road`** — the mile of road between the crossing and the ford. The author's "if the
budget allows and the walk wants a middle" is taken, and the walk does want a middle: without
it, EAST at the crossing puts the player in the river instantly and the mile between the
ninth stone and the eighth is a claim the game never makes them feel. It gets **two** faces,
2099 BA and 99 AA — the two ages the crossing exists in, which are the two ages the walk
happens in. Its time exits are **closed in both**, exactly as `high-crossing`'s are: this is
road, and the loose ground around here is the House, its dooryard and the riverbank. Nobody
remarks on it and the narrator never explains it. The engine prints no time line when both
sides are closed, so there is nothing to explain away.

The player never has to type FUTURE to reach the river: in 2099 BA, OUT · EAST · EAST · EAST
does it, which is the author's requirement met by walking alone.

### The two edits to existing rooms

Two lines, in two files, and nothing else anywhere outside `src/content/`'s new files:

| File | add |
| --- | --- |
| `src/content/high-crossing-2099-ba.ts` | `east: "east-road:2099-ba"` in the existing `exits` |
| `src/content/high-crossing-99-aa.ts` | `east: "east-road:99-aa"` in the existing `exits` |

No prose, no items, no scenery, no `talk` line, no `time` flag in either file is to be
touched. The yard faces and the House faces are not to be opened at all.

**A discoverability note, recorded and not acted on.** The engine never lists spatial exits;
a player finds an exit because the prose named it. Neither `high-crossing` face's `look`
currently names anything east — 2099 BA ends on *"The House shows one lit window back west"*,
99 AA on *"The houses are back west."* — and both `look` bodies are already at the voice
lint's four-sentence ceiling, so a sentence cannot be added and the issue forbids editing a
clause. The exit therefore rests on what is already shipped: both faces carry `crossing`
scenery describing **two** roads fitted into each other, the mason's `talk` in 2099 BA says
*"Nine miles, and the last two of them are mine,"* and a player standing at a crossroads
typing EAST is doing the obvious thing. The generator must leave it there. If the
orchestrator later wants the road east named in one clause, that is a one-line decision for a
separate issue, not something the generator may take on itself.

### The eighth stone, exactly

The stone carries two lines and nothing else:

```
ELLERMARK
EIGHT
```

Same town, same hand, one mile nearer to it than the ninth. Words, not numerals, exactly as
the ninth is written.

**Write it once.** `src/content/high-crossing-2099-ba.ts` already exports
`MILESTONE_LETTERS = "ELLERMARK\nNINE"` and every face that shows the ninth stone imports it
rather than retyping it. Do the same here: export a single constant — `EIGHTH_STONE_LETTERS`
— from `src/content/ford-2099-ba.ts`, the first face written, and **import** it into all five
later ford faces. Byte-identity across six ages then follows from the module graph and not
from anybody's proofreading. Weathering, weed, scrubbing and silt live in each face's
`description`, which is the only place they can live if the `read` strings are to stay one
string.

Do not derive one constant from the other, and do not build either from parts. Two plain
literals in two files, spelled the same way, is what "the same hand" means here.

Where the stone is, age by age. It is an `Item` with `takeable: false` in all six faces —
`read` is an `Item` field and `Scenery` has not got one, so anything the player reads must be
an `Item`.

| Landing · age | Where it is | What it is being used as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2099 BA · the High Masonry | Standing at the head of the stepping stones, new-cut and pale along the letters, its foot under water at this level. | A bollard for the ferry line, and something for the tollman to set his box on. |
| 1099 BA · the Long Noon | Four feet out in the shallows; the river has moved, the stone has not. Green to the waterline, letters above it and readable. | A depth gauge. The ferryman reads the water off it twice a day. |
| 99 BA · the Hush | Clear of the water altogether, foot and all, for the first time in an age. | Nothing. There is nobody here to use it. |
| 99 AA · the Morning Country | Down in the silt at the narrows, with the near end of the plank resting on it. | A plank-rest. The village calls it the plank stone. |
| 1099 AA · the Rekindling | Built into the near wall of the lock chamber, face out, at the waterline. | A course of masonry and a gauge. The keeper charges by what the water does against its top line. |
| 2099 AA · the Lettered Age | Out of the wall, scrubbed, upright on a plinth beside the towpath, under a printed label. | An exhibit, catalogued by where it was found. |

**Nobody says the word.** Not the tollman, not the ferryman, not the eel-woman, not the
lock-keeper, not the label, not the narrator. The word `EIGHT` appears in the game exactly
once, as the second line of `EIGHTH_STONE_LETTERS`, and only a player who types READ ever
sees it. Every `talk` line about the stone is about water or money.

**The label in 2099 AA** is a separate `Item` with its own `read`, in the small-capitals
museum register the yard's placard already uses. It must be careful, sourced and wrong in a
new way — not a second helping of the same joke. The shape that works: it dates the stone to
the Rekindling **because it came out of a Rekindling lock wall**, names it a lock gauge,
transcribes the first line as a place-name of uncertain application, and files the second
line as an uninterpreted mason's tally. It does not quote the word on the second line, it
does not mention the crossing, and it does not mention a sequence. A mile west and a thousand
years of scholarship away, the University's other placard is telling visitors that the other
eight stones have never been found. Neither text knows about the other. The narrator does not
introduce them.

### The price of the crossing

The second through-line, and the author's instruction that "toll prices and what the
lock-keeper charges are information, not mood" made into a structure. Somebody has charged
for this water in most ages, and the price is always given as a fact, the way you would give
the time. It never becomes a puzzle and the player never needs a coin.

| Landing | The price |
| --- | --- |
| 2099 BA | A toll-board nailed to the ferry-post: a rate for a cart, a rate for a beast, and foot passage at half of that. New road, new prices. |
| 1099 BA | The toll is a lease now, held for a term of years and turning a profit. The rate has gone up and nobody argues about it, because nobody in the Long Noon argues about money. |
| 99 BA | The box is on the post and it is empty, and the man is not here. |
| 99 AA | Nothing. The plank is free and nobody alive knows that it was ever otherwise. |
| 1099 AA | The lock-keeper's rate, per boat and per lockful, entered in a book and chalked on a slate — and these are the tolls already chalked up daily at the Turning House in this age, which is shipped canon at `turning-house:1099-aa`. |
| 2099 AA | The lock is free and the exhibit asks for a contribution. The toll has come off the water and gone onto the stone. |

### The river

Facts the generator must build on rather than reinvent, because they are already shipped:

- **The river is never named.** The maps of the Long Noon call it "the river" and get it
  wrong twice — `long-noon-map` at `turning-house:1099-ba` "puts the river on the wrong side
  of the hills, in good ink," and `carter-map` at `crossroads-yard:1099-ba` has "the river on
  it twice, in two places." Both are jokes about *this* river and the 1099 BA ford face may
  lean on them in one clause. Naming the river would be a new proper noun and this story does
  not need one; **Ellermark**, already canon, is the only proper noun in it.
- **The lock is canon.** `turning-house:1099-aa` carries the scenery line "Canal tolls
  chalked up daily, since the river got a lock and stopped arguing." The lock at the ford is
  that lock. This story is showing the player something the House has been mentioning.
- **The canal is a separate cut.** `crossroads-yard:1099-aa` puts the canal "a mile north,
  with the barges going at the pace of a walking horse." The lock in this story is on the
  **river**, at the ford, one mile east; the canal meets the river below it. That is one
  clause at most and probably none. Do not move the canal to the ford and do not call the
  river a canal.
- **The water level changes and the stone does not.** Highest in the Long Noon, lowest in the
  Hush, silted in the Morning Country, held by gates in the Rekindling, measured in the
  Lettered Age. Every level is stated as a measurement or a fact about what it means for
  passage, never as weather-with-feelings.

### The edge of the map

There is no room on the far bank and there never will be one in this story. No ford face has
an `east` exit in any age.

The engine's reply to a direction with no exit is the fixed `You can't go that way.`, and
there is no verb for SWIM, CROSS, WADE or ROW — the parser answers an unknown verb with
`I don't know the word "swim".`, which is the genre's own dry refusal and is already in
voice. Neither reply can be authored per room, so **the in-voice work is done by scenery**:
every ford face carries one piece of far-bank scenery whose single-sentence `description` is
the refusal, in that age's own terms. A reason, never a wall:

| Landing | Why you are not crossing today |
| --- | --- |
| 2099 BA | The water is over the last three stepping stones and the ferryman will not take anything across at this level. |
| 1099 BA | The lease says the boat runs at high water, and the man holding the lease says this is not high water. |
| 99 BA | The far bank has the road going on over it and nothing on it at all, in either direction, which has never happened. |
| 99 AA | The plank ends on a silt bar, and the channel past the bar is deeper than the plank is long. |
| 1099 AA | The gates are shut, and the keeper opens them for boats. |
| 2099 AA | The gates are chained for the season and the towpath ends at the plinth. |

The prose must never offer the far bank as a direction, and no face may describe a way over
that the player could reasonably expect to take.

### People

Four, across eight rooms, and never two in a room. Everwyn must not feel crowded — this is
the same density the previous story shipped. All are `Scenery` carrying a `talk` line; the
engine has no NPC type and scenery with `talk` is how it is done. All speak plainly: era
colour is in what they know, never in how they conjugate.

- **The tollman**, `ford:2099-ba`. Sitting at the ferry-post with a box. Gives the price and
  the depth, in that order, and has nothing to say about the stone beyond what he keeps on
  it. The road is a week old and he is already bored of it.
- **The ferryman**, `ford:1099-ba`. Holds the lease and does well out of it. Reads the water
  off the stone twice a day, has done for twenty years, and could not tell you there are
  letters on it.
- **The eel-woman**, `ford:99-aa`. Setting a trap in the slack water by the plank. Calls the
  stone the plank stone and tells the player what it is for, kindly and with certainty. She
  cannot read and neither can anyone she knows, which she does not experience as a lack.
- **The lock-keeper**, `ford:1099-aa`. In the cottage doorway with the book. Quotes the rate,
  the lockful and what the water is doing against the mark. Proud of the gates and indifferent
  to the wall they are set in.

`ford:99-ba`, `ford:2099-aa` and both `east-road` faces have **nobody in them**. The Hush is
empty because that is what the Hush is. The Lettered Age's ford is empty because the label is
the voice in that room, and because the previous story already ended on a student at a
placard — ending this one on printed text and no reader is the sharper version, not a repeat.

### Tone notes

Dry on the surface, deep underneath. The water level, the toll prices, the lease terms and
the lock-keeper's rate are **information, not mood**. A silt bar is a silt bar. The narrator
does not know that a stone used as a plank-rest is a symbol of anything, and must not find
out.

No line concludes on the player's behalf. Nobody explains the Gap, nobody explains why a road
stops being a road, and nobody anywhere connects the two stones. The story's whole payload is
a connection the text refuses to make.

Era inflection is in the **nouns**: stepping stones, ferry-post, toll-board, lease, plank,
eel-trap, lock gates, lockful, gauge board, benchmark, level peg, plinth. One narrator, one
voice, first landing to last. No pastiche grammar anywhere, `talk` lines included.

History in one clause, never a paragraph. The Morning Country face does not narrate what
happened to the ferry; it says silt has the stones and lays a plank across.

### What the engine can and cannot do here

Binding constraints, confirmed against `src/types.ts`, `src/world.ts`, `src/engine.ts`,
`src/parser.ts` and the previous story's as-built notes. Build within them.

- **A stride needs a face at the adjacent landing.** `strideTarget` (`src/world.ts:15`)
  resolves to the same `place` at the neighbouring entry in `world.landings`, and to nothing
  otherwise. An open flag with no face on the other side gives the player *"You reach on. The
  years give — but nothing of this place stands in that age, and they set you down where you
  were."* **This plan has no such case.** Keep it that way; the table above says why each
  flag is what it is.
- **Spatial exits are never auto-listed.** The engine prints only its own time line. If the
  prose does not name a road or a plank, the player will never find the exit. Weave exits
  into the `look` per the writing guide's room template, never as a compass line.
- **Exits are not reciprocal automatically.** Every `east` needs its matching `west`.
- **The engine validates at startup and rejects an exit pointing at a missing room**, so an
  exit and the room it points at must land in the same commit. The build order below is
  arranged around that.
- **No world state, no flags, no conditional text.** An item's `description` and `read` are
  one fixed string for the whole game. Nothing can react to what the player carries or has
  done. Do not write a toll that wants paying or a lock that wants working.
- **Verbs that exist:** look, examine, take, drop, inventory, go, past, future, when, wait,
  again, say, talk, mark, read, eat, help, quit. Directions include `in`/`out`. There is no
  `OPEN`, `PUT`, `GIVE`, `PAY`, `CROSS`, `SWIM` or `CLIMB`.
- **`read` is an `Item` field only.** `Scenery` has `description` and `talk`. Anything the
  player must read is an `Item` with `takeable: false` and a dry `takeRefusal`.
- **Item and scenery ids are globally unique across the world.** Suffix every new id with its
  landing slug. Bare names already taken include `milestone-2099-ba`, `milestone-1099-ba`,
  `milestone-99-ba`, `milestone-99-aa`, `milestone-1099-aa`, `milestone-2099-aa`, `tolls`,
